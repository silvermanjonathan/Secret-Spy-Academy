The Unbreakable Wheel

Day 2's afternoon taught you the leak: count the top letter and the shift confesses. For three hundred years there was a wheel the leak could not touch. Its trick is one idea: give every letter its own key. Today you build it, break your own leak against it, and meet its self-turning cousin.

Drill 1 · The marching keyE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Every letter gets its own shift

The keyword FOX marches beside the message: the first letter shifts by F's slot (5), the second by O's (14), the third by X's (23), and then the keyword starts over. The cycling is one mark you own: i % len(keyword) walks 0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2 forever. This is the Vigenère cipher, and for three centuries its nickname was the unbreakable one.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "THE EAGLE FLIES AT MIDNIGHT"
keyword = "FOX"
result = ""
i = 0
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for j in range(27):
        if rack[j] == letter:
            slot = j
    kletter = keyword[i % len(keyword)]
    kslot = 0
    for j in range(27):
        if rack[j] == kletter:
            kslot = j
    result = result + rack[(slot + kslot) % 27]
    i = i + 1
print(result)
Predict Trace the first three letters by hand. T (slot 19) plus F (slot 5) lands where? H (7) plus O (14)? E (4) plus X (23), which wraps? And which key letter does the fourth message letter use?
T plus F is slot 24, Y. H plus O is slot 21, V. E plus X is 27, and 27 % 27 is 0: A, a full turn landing back at the start. The fourth letter uses F again, because 3 % 3 is 0 and the march restarts. Notice what just happened: the message has three Es, and they encode as A, then other letters entirely, because each met a different key letter. The leak counts letters; this wheel makes the same letter wear different masks.

Drill 2 · Decode by complementE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The keys that undo, marching in step

Day 2's decode law scales up: every key has a complement that sums to 27. To decode, march the same keyword and shift by each key letter's complement, 27 - kslot.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "YVAESXLZAETHNSOEOPE EIAELVP"
keyword = "FOX"
result = ""
i = 0
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for j in range(27):
        if rack[j] == letter:
            slot = j
    kletter = keyword[i % len(keyword)]
    kslot = 0
    for j in range(27):
        if rack[j] == kletter:
            kslot = j
    result = result + rack[(slot + 27 - kslot) % 27]
    i = i + 1
print(result)
Predict Exactly one line differs from Drill 1. Which one? And F's complement is 22 while X's is 4: why does a bigger key letter get a smaller undo?
The result line: (slot + kslot) became (slot + 27 - kslot). And the see-saw is the wheel itself: a shift of 23 forward is 4 short of a full turn, so 4 more finishes the loop and lands you home. Forward plus complement is always 27, one full turn, no motion at all. Encode and decode are the same machine walking the circle in the same direction, one finishing the other's lap.

Drill 3 · The leak, defeatedE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Your own weapon bounces off

On Day 2 you counted eight Hs in a Caesar message and unmasked shift 3. Run the same count against the same message wearing Vigenère armor, keyword FOX.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
caesar = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
armored = "RSAYNIJNXYNPMSWXOBJNDTHOJNXYNOJIAS"
count = 0
for letter in caesar:
    if letter == "H":
        count = count + 1
print("H in the Caesar text:", count)
count = 0
for letter in armored:
    if letter == "N":
        count = count + 1
print("N in the armored text:", count)
Predict Both texts hide the same message. The armored text's most common letter is N, with the count you're about to see. Apply Day 2's trick to it: if N were E in disguise, the shift would be 13 minus 4, so 9. Will decoding the armored text as a Caesar shift of 9 reveal the message?
Eight against six, and the six is a lie. Decode the armored text as shift 9 and out comes gibberish, because there is no single shift to find: those six Ns came from different plain letters that happened to meet the right key letters. The leak works by assuming one mask for the whole message; give every position its own mask and the count points nowhere. This is why the wheel held for three hundred years, until Babbage and Kasiski found a deeper leak: the keyword itself repeats, every three letters here, and patience plus that rhythm cracks it. No armor is forever; good armor just raises the price.

Drill 4 · The rotorE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The wheel that turns itself

Vigenère needs a keyword. The rotor needs only a starting key: after every letter, the wheel clicks forward one notch on its own, so the shift is (key + i) % 27. This is the seed of the Enigma machine, the self-turning cipher an entire war was fought over.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "MEET AT DAWN"
key = 3
result = ""
i = 0
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for j in range(27):
        if rack[j] == letter:
            slot = j
    result = result + rack[(slot + (key + i) % 27) % 27]
    i = i + 1
print(result)
Predict The message opens M, E, E. The M shifts by 3. By how much does each E shift, and will the two Es come out as the same letter?
The first E shifts by 4 and becomes I; the second shifts by 5 and becomes J. Same letter in, different letters out, and no keyword was ever agreed: the clicking is the key schedule. The count-the-top-letter leak dies against this wheel too, for the same reason as Drill 3. Decoding is Drill 2's move: undo each position with 27 - (key + i) % 27, the complement of wherever the rotor stood.

Where does the march stand?

Part 1: keyword FOX, message position i = 7. Which key letter does that position use? Work 7 % 3 first.
Part 2: a rival team armors a drop with keyword GO, a two-letter march. Is their armor stronger or weaker than FOX's three-letter march, and why?

Part 1: O. 7 % 3 is 1, and slot 1 of the keyword is its second letter. If you said X, you counted the keyword from 1; its slots start at 0 like every rack this week. Part 2: weaker. A two-letter march means every other letter shares a mask, so the message is really two Caesar ciphers braided together, and Day 2's count cracks each half. The keyword's length is the armor's thickness; the pad in the next mission takes that idea to its limit.
Field Mission · Armor the Drop

Your keyword, your wheel

Part A, armor it: your own message (capitals and spaces), your own keyword of three or more letters, through Drill 1's machine. Write the coded text and the keyword on separate slips.

Part B, the exchange: hand a classmate the coded slip, then speak the keyword. Their Drill 2 machine opens it. The keyword is the key ceremony's lesson wearing armor: the machine was public all along.

Part C, stretch: run Drill 3's count against your own armored drop and show the top letter lies. Then rotor your own name with your birthday's day-of-month as the key.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "DROP AT THE OLD PIER"
keyword = "RAVEN"
result = ""
i = 0
for letter in message:

    i = i + 1

Decode is the same program with (slot + 27 - kslot) % 27 on the result line, exactly as in Drill 2.

Standards, pacing & evidence (teacher panel)

Where this fits: the flagship of the Cryptography Wing, forty-five minutes, terminal only, for agents who finished both Day 2 benches; % from Drill 10 is load-bearing here. The mission is a narrative sequel: Day 2 handed recruits the leak, this page hands them the armor, and Drill 3 stages the collision. The history is real and worth telling: Vigenère's wheel was called le chiffre indéchiffrable for three centuries, and Babbage and Kasiski broke it by attacking the keyword's rhythm, which the Drill 3 reveal names without flinching.

Where recruits actually stall: the march itself: recruits who write the keyword letters above the message letters on paper before running Drill 1 almost never stall, and recruits who skip the paper march confuse i (the message position) with j (the search index). The hinge's Part 1 catches keyword-counting-from-1. In Part B of the build, the classic failure is a keyword spoken with a spelling mistake, which is its own lesson about key exchange.
