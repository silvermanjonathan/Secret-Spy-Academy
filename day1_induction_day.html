Secret Spy Academy · Day 1

Induction Day & the Three Clearance Checks

Your field terminal arrived today. It's obedient and completely literal: it does exactly what you type, which is not always what you meant. Get your codename on file, then pass the three clearance checks: Transmit, Patrol, Checkpoint.

Drill 1 · First transmission

Bring the terminal online

Type this one line into day1.py, exactly as written. Press Run (the triangle, top right) and watch the terminal.

print("HQ to Recruit: your terminal is live.")
Predict What exact characters appear? Will the quotation marks be part of it? Say your guess out loud, then run.
The words appear without the quotation marks. Quotes are instructions for the terminal: they mark where your message starts and ends. Everything between them is called a string.
You should see 
HQ to Recruit: your terminal is live.
Drill 2 · Sabotage drill: the misfireE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

Break it, then read the incident report

Every agent fumbles a transmission sooner or later. Do it on purpose now, so the error message can never rattle you again. Change print to pritn and run:

pritn("HQ to Recruit: your terminal is live.")
Predict Will anything print? What will the terminal say instead, and where on the screen will you look first?
Nothing prints. The terminal files an incident report instead, called a traceback. Read it last line first: the bottom line names the problem, and here it says the terminal knows no command called pritn. Fix the spelling, run again, and the transmission goes through.
You should see (last line of the traceback) 
NameError: name 'pritn' is not defined. Did you mean: 'print'?
Drill 3 · The labeled briefcaseE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Put a value inside, read the tag

A variable is a labeled briefcase: the name on the tag, one value inside. Watch the two prints closely. One has quotes and one doesn't.

codename = "Falcon"
print(codename)
print("codename")
Predict Two lines will appear. What exactly does each one say? Write both in your Field Notebook before you run.
First Falcon, then codename. Without quotes, the terminal opens the briefcase and shows what's inside. With quotes, it just repeats those exact letters. This one difference explains half the misfires you'll see this week.
You should see 
Falcon
codename
Drill 4 · One briefcase, one thingE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Swap the contents

What happens when you put a second value into a briefcase that already holds one?

clearance = 2
clearance = 5
print(clearance)
Predict Will the terminal show 2, 5, both, or 7? Trace it in your Field Notebook, one row per line.
5, and only 5. The = sign swaps the contents: whatever the briefcase held before is gone, and the most recent swap wins. Nothing adds up unless you ask for math on purpose.
You should see 
5
Drill 5 · The intercomE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Make the terminal ask a question

input() makes the program stop and listen. Whatever gets typed lands in the briefcase on the left.

codename = input("State your codename, Recruit: ")
print("Welcome aboard,", codename)
Predict Where exactly does the program pause and wait? After you type a codename and press Enter, what ends up inside codename?
It pauses at line 1, right after printing the question. The blinking cursor in the terminal is the intercom listening. Whatever you type before pressing Enter becomes the value inside codename, and line 2 reads it back out. The comma inside print puts one space between the two pieces.
You should see (if the recruit is Nightjar) 
State your codename, Recruit: Nightjar
Welcome aboard, Nightjar
Drill 6 · The text trap, and decoding on arrivalE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

Why 7 × 4 makes 7777, and how int() fixes it

Ask for a number, multiply it, and watch closely. Type 7 when it asks. Then run the fixed version and type 7 again.

missions = input("How many missions completed? ")
print(missions * 4)

# decode on arrival: the fix
missions = int(input("How many missions completed? "))
print(f"Field points: {missions * 4}")
Predict First run: 28, or something stranger? Second run: what changes, and what does the f before the quotes do?
First: 7777. input() always hands you text, and text times 4 repeats the letters. The terminal did exactly what you said. Second: int() converts the text to a real number the moment it arrives, so the math works. The Academy calls this decoding on arrival. And the f makes an f-string: anything inside { } gets swapped for its value.
You should see (typing 7 both times) 
How many missions completed? 7
7777
How many missions completed? 7
Field points: 28
Drill 7 · Patrol check: for and rangeE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

One rule, many rows

A loop repeats your indented lines, and the briefcase step is refilled each pass. Hand it a rule and a shape appears. Here a tracker's signal climbs one bar per step.

print("|" * 5)

for step in range(1, 5):
    print("|" * step)
Predict Line 1 first: five bars, or the number 5? Then the loop: sketch every row in your Field Notebook. Where does range(1, 5) start, and where does it stop?
Five bars, then a staircase of 1 to 4. Text times a number repeats the text, and range(1, 5) deals 1, 2, 3, 4: it starts at the first number and stops before the second. One small rule made every row. The loop is a counted patrol: you can say in advance exactly how many passes it makes.
You should see 
|||||
|
||
|||

Drill 8 · Sabotage drill: the missing rowE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The off-by-one

A recruit wants a four-row signal and writes this. Read it first. Something is short.

for step in range(1, 4):
    print("|" * step)
Predict How many rows will actually print, and which row is missing? Say why before you run.
Three rows. The four-bar row never prints, because range(1, 4) stops before 4. This is the most famous misfire in programming, the off-by-one. The fix: to reach the number you want, write one past it, range(1, 5).
You should see (one row short) 
|
||
|||
Drill 9 · Checkpoint check: if and elseE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

The two-lane checkpoint

An if line asks a yes-or-no question. The indented lines below it run only when the answer is yes, and else catches every no.

signal = 80
if signal > 75:
    print("Lock acquired. Tracker on target.")
else:
    print("No lock. Move closer.")
Predict Which one line prints at 80? Change signal to 60 and predict again before the second run. Could both lines ever print on the same run?
At 80, the lock. At 60, no lock. And no: exactly one lane opens per run. The terminal takes one lane through the checkpoint and moves on.
You should see (with signal = 80) 
Lock acquired. Tracker on target.
Drill 10 · Three lanes, flatE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

elif: the middle lane

Three outcomes, one flat ladder. The terminal checks each question in order and takes the first true lane.

threat = 90
if threat < 30:
    print("All quiet. Proceed.")
elif threat > 80:
    print("Abort. Get out now.")
else:
    print("Caution. Keep your head down.")
Predict At 90: how many of the three lines print, and which one? What about at exactly 80?
Exactly one line, always. At 90 it's Abort. At 80 both questions are false (80 is not less than 30, and 80 is not greater than 80), so the else lane opens: Caution. Notice the ladder stays flat, three lanes side by side, none hiding inside another. That's how checkpoints are written at this Academy.
You should see (with threat = 90) 
Abort. Get out now.
Drill 11 · The gadget rackE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

A list: one briefcase, many slots

A list is a rack of numbered slots, and the numbering starts at 0. Then reach for a slot that isn't there, on purpose.

rack = ["grapple", "decoder", "smoke pen"]
print(rack[0])
print(rack[2])
print(len(rack))
print(rack[3])
Predict Three lines will print, and then something else happens. Which gadget comes out of slot 0? What does len report? And slot 3: the last gadget, or a misfire?
grapple, smoke pen, then 3, and then the terminal misfires. The slots are numbered 0, 1, 2, so slot 3 doesn't exist. On a three-slot rack the last slot is number 2. The last number is always one less than the count. Counting from zero feels strange at first; draw the rack in your Field Notebook with the slot numbers on it and it sticks.
You should see (then the traceback's last line) 
grapple
smoke pen
3
IndexError: list index out of range
Drill 12 · The Dispatcher and the checkpointE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

random.choice and in: the rack comes alive

Import the Dispatcher once, at the top of the file. random.choice picks a slot for you, and the word in asks the rack a yes-or-no question. Run it twice: request decoder, then jetpack.

import random

rack = ["grapple", "decoder", "smoke pen"]
print("The Dispatcher issues:", random.choice(rack))

ask = input("Which gadget do you request? ")
if ask in rack:
    print("Approved. Sign it out at the rack.")
else:
    print("Denied. That gadget is not on the rack.")
Predict What lands in the Dispatcher's pick: a gadget, or a slot number? Which lane opens for decoder, and which for jetpack? What would typing Decoder with a capital D do?
A gadget. The Dispatcher hands you the word itself, and slot numbers stay on the rack. The pick can repeat across runs; each one is fresh and forgetful. decoder opens the approved lane, jetpack hits the denied lane, and Decoder is denied too: the terminal matches letters exactly, and a capital D is a different letter.
You should see (two runs; the Dispatcher's pick may differ) 
The Dispatcher issues: smoke pen
Which gadget do you request? decoder
Approved. Sign it out at the rack.

The Dispatcher issues: grapple
Which gadget do you request? jetpack
Denied. That gadget is not on the rack.
Hinge question · stop here until you're sureE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The two-part clearance check

Part 1: a program runs fuel = 40 and then fuel = 25. What does fuel hold now?
Part 2: without running it, how many lines does for step in range(2, 5): print("|" * step) print, and how many bars are on the last line?

Part 1: 25, and only 25. A briefcase holds one value, and = swaps out whatever was there. Part 2: three lines, and the last holds four bars. range(2, 5) deals 2, 3, 4. If either part surprised you, re-trace Drills 4 and 7 before the build. If you got both, you're cleared for the Field Mission.
Field Mission · The Induction Terminal

Register, get scored, get assigned

Build an Induction Terminal program that:

1. Prints the HQ banner (2–3 lines, or a loop of one banner line).
2. Asks the recruit's codename with input().
3. Asks how many missions they've completed, converting with int().
4. Computes their field points (each mission is worth 15) and prints one f-string report line.
5. Gates the result: over 45 points earns one message, otherwise another.
6. Lets the Dispatcher assign a Division with random.choice.

Useful pieces: print() · input() · int() · f"..." · for / range() · if / else · random.choice()

Suggested start: banner first, then one question at a time. House law: one change per run.

import random

# --- HQ banner ---
for row in range(3):
    print("= = = = = = = = = = =")

# --- Induction desk ---
codename = input("State your codename, Recruit: ")
missions = int(input("How many missions completed? "))
points = missions * 15
print(f"{codename}, your file shows {points} field points.")

# --- The clearance checkpoint ---
if points > 45:
    print("Seasoned already. HQ will be watching you.")
else:
    print("A clean slate. Every mission adds to it.")

# --- The Dispatcher assigns ---
division = random.choice(["Surveillance", "Cryptography", "Field Ops"])
print(f"The Dispatcher assigns you to {division}.")
Yours doesn't have to match this. It has to run, and you have to be able to say what each line does.

Choose your exit gate

Floor · approaching the standard

Your banner prints, your terminal asks for a codename and a mission count with int(), and you can sabotage print on purpose and fix it by reading the incident report last line first.

Middle · meets the standard

Your full Induction Terminal runs, checkpoint and Division assignment included, and your Field Notebook trace of the codename, missions, and points program is complete and correct.

Stretch · exceeds the standard

Standards, pacing & evidence (teacher panel)

The pacing choice, stated plainly: Day 1 is a deliberate fire-hose, about ten mechanics in twelve drills. Small self-confirming moves (printing, assigning, indexing) compress and hold; abstraction doesn't, so Day 3 is a reading day and Days 4 and 5 introduce nothing new. Total load stays roughly flat across the week because integration rises as novelty falls. The floor tier here is a complete, runnable program.

Where recruits actually stall: Block A (Drills 1 to 6) runs about 45 minutes, and the text trap in Drill 6 is the first real stall, usually a recruit who typed the number with quotes or dropped the int() wrapper. Block B (Drills 7 to 12 plus the hinge) runs about 50 minutes; zero-indexing in Drill 11 and the exact-boundary case in Drill 10 (80 is not greater than 80) are the two predictable stalls. Block C, the Field Mission and the Mission Debrief, needs a full 40 minutes. Protect it.
