Drill 1 · The cipher rackE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

A word is a rack of letters

Yesterday a rack held gadgets. Text is a rack too: every character has a slot number, starting at 0. This rack holds the 26 capital letters and one space, 27 slots. Two new questions come with it: == asks "the same?" and != asks "different?" The terminal answers True or False.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
print(rack[0], rack[4], len(rack))
print(rack[7] == "H")
print(rack[7] == "h")
print(rack[7] != "h")
Predict Three things print on the first line, then three answers. Write all of them. Which slot holds the space?
A E 27, then True, False, True. Slot 7 is H, and a small h is a different letter: the terminal matches exactly (Day 1, Drill 12's rule). The space lives in slot 26, the last one, and it will matter this afternoon. Read != aloud as "is not" and it stops being slippery. One = fills a briefcase; two ask a question.

Drill 2 · The slot searchE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Which slot is this letter in?

A cipher wheel needs a letter's slot number. The program walks every slot with Day 1's patrol and asks the same question at each one; when the gate says yes, it remembers where it was.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
letter = "H"
slot = 0
for i in range(27):
    if rack[i] == letter:
        slot = i
print("Slot of", letter, "is", slot)
Predict How many times does the gate say yes across all 27 passes? What does slot hold at the end, and what would it hold if letter were a small "h"?
Yes exactly once, on pass 7, so slot ends at 7. A small "h" is on no rack, so the gate never says yes and slot keeps its starting 0: the program would quietly treat an unknown letter as an A. The shape, a loop with a gate inside and a briefcase filled by the gate, is the counter with a gate's cousin; you'll meet the counter itself in Drill 7.
You should see 
Slot of H is 7
Drill 3 · The shift and the wrapE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Past the end of the rack, come back around

A shift cipher moves every letter the same number of slots forward. Past slot 26 there is no slot 27, so the wheel comes back around to the start. That's a flat gate doing the wrapping.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
slot = 24
shift = 5
slot = slot + shift
if slot > 26:
    slot = slot - 27
print(slot, rack[slot])
Predict Slot 24 is Y. Trace it: what number and what letter print? What would print with shift = 1? And if a saboteur changed the 27 to 26, which letters would come out wrong: all of them, or only some?
2 C: 24 plus 5 is 29, the gate fires, 29 minus 27 is 2, and slot 2 is C. With shift 1 the sum is 25, the gate stays shut, and Z prints. The sabotaged 26 would be a silent bug: only letters that wrap would land one slot too far, and the program would never complain. Keep that idea; it's Thursday's kind of bug.
You should see 
2 C
Drill 4 · Pattern: the tallyE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

A briefcase that collects, one pass at a time

The most useful pattern in programming: start a briefcase empty, then let a loop add into it, one piece per pass. Programmers call it the accumulator; at this Academy it's the tally. Here it collects letters; with numbers it becomes a running total, which is how the capstone game will count the passes you hold your lock on target.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
result = ""
for i in range(5):
    result = result + rack[i]
    print(result)
Predict Trace result through all five passes in your Field Notebook. What prints on pass 3, and what does the last line show?
Pass 3 prints ABC; the last line is ABCDE. The line result = result + rack[i] reads strangely at first: put into the briefcase its own contents plus one more letter. The empty quotes at the top matter: a tally has to start with something to add to.

Drill 5 · The Cipher WheelE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Encode: the slot search, the shift, the wrap, the tally

Drills 2, 3, and 4 stacked into one machine. One new move: for letter in message: walks a word letter by letter, the way Day 1's loop walked a rack. Inside it, the slot search runs for each letter, so this is a loop inside a loop. Capitals and spaces only.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "MEET ME AT THE SAFE HOUSE AT SEVEN"
shift = 3
result = ""
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for i in range(27):
        if rack[i] == letter:
            slot = i
    slot = slot + shift
    if slot > 26:
        slot = slot - 27
    result = result + rack[slot]
print(result)
Predict Trace the first three letters, M, E, E, through the wheel. What do they become? And what does a space turn into with shift 3?
M (slot 12) becomes P, and both Es (slot 4) become H, so the coded text begins PHH. The space is slot 26; 26 plus 3 is 29, the wrap brings it to 2, and it comes out as C. Every C in the coded text is a space in disguise, which is why the coded line has no spaces at all. Julius Caesar used this exact cipher two thousand years ago; the wheel in your terminal is the same machine.

Drill 6 · DecodeE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Going forward 24 is going back 3

There is no separate decoder. On a wheel of 27 slots, moving forward 24 lands exactly where moving back 3 would. Feed the coded text back into Drill 5 with the complementary shift. Two lines change; everything else is the same machine.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
shift = 24
result = ""
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for i in range(27):
        if rack[i] == letter:
            slot = i
    slot = slot + shift
    if slot > 26:
        slot = slot - 27
    result = result + rack[slot]
print(result)
Predict Why 24? A message encoded with shift 10 arrives: what shift decodes it, and how do you know without running anything?
Because 3 plus 24 is 27, one full turn of the wheel, so every letter lands back where it started. A shift of 10 is undone by 17, for the same reason. The rule: the decoding shift is 27 minus the encoding shift. A program that does a new job after one deliberate change is a program you understand.

Drill 7 · Frequency analysisE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Pattern: the counter with a gate

Codebreakers have a trick that needs no key. In English, E is the most common letter. Count the letters in a coded message and the most common one is probably E in disguise. The tool is a tally that adds 1 instead of a letter, and only when a gate says yes.

coded = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
count = 0
for letter in coded:
    if letter == "H":
        count = count + 1
print("H appears", count, "times")
Predict How many Hs are in the coded text? If H is really E in disguise, what does that tell you about the shift that made this message?
Eight. And H sits three slots past E, so a codebreaker who has never seen the key guesses shift 3 from the count alone. Count C next and you'll find seven: the spaces in disguise, the second giveaway. Real agents strip the spaces out before encoding, for exactly this reason. The counter with a gate is three Day 1 tools in one shape: a loop, a checkpoint inside it, and a briefcase swap inside the checkpoint.
You should see 
H appears 8 times
Drill 8 · Meet whileE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The big shift: watch, don't count

A shift of 60 goes around the wheel twice and then some. Drill 3's gate wraps once and stops caring. while is different: it checks its question at the top of every pass and keeps running the indented lines while the answer is yes. for counts; while watches.

slot = 60
while slot > 26:
    slot = slot - 27
    print(slot)
print("Landed on slot", slot)
Predict Trace it in your Field Notebook, one row per check, including the check that fails. What numbers print, and when does the last line finally run?
33, then 6, then Landed on slot 6. Three checks: 60 is more than 26, yes; 33 is more than 26, yes; 6 is not, no, and only then does the last line run. Every while ends on a check that fails. Notice the subtraction is Drill 4's tally running backward; a while is often a tally with a finish line.

Landed on slot 6
Drill 9 · Sabotage drill: the runaway loopE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The loop that never stops, and how to stop it

A while must move toward no. Break that law on purpose, once, so you know the fix. Before you run this, find the stop control: the square stop button in the terminal's corner, or click in the terminal and press Ctrl+C.

slot = 60
while slot > 26:
    slot = slot + 27
    print(slot)
Predict Read the question and the line under it. Will the answer ever become no? What is going to happen when you run this?
Never. slot starts above 26 and only grows, so the question stays yes forever and numbers pour down the terminal until you end it. Stop it now. This is the runaway loop, and every programmer sets one off by accident sometimes. The fix is a habit: before running any while, point at the line that moves it toward no. If you can't find one, don't run it.
You should see 
87, 114, 141, 168... climbing forever until you press stop.
Then the terminal reports the program was interrupted.
Drill 10 · Pattern: the sentinel gateE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The Safe House door: ask until the passphrase is spoken

The classic while pattern: keep asking while the answer is wrong. The exit is a word, not a count, and it's Drill 1's != doing the watching.

passphrase = "nightjar"
guess = input("Passphrase: ")
while guess != passphrase:
    print("The door stays shut.")
    guess = input("Passphrase: ")
print("The door unlocks. Come in.")
Predict Guess wrong twice, then right. How many times does "The door stays shut." print? Which line moves the loop toward no?
Twice, once per wrong guess. The line that moves it toward no is the second input(), inside the loop: it refills guess so the question can change its answer. Delete that line and the door asks once and sulks forever. A passphrase is the simplest key there is: the door doesn't decode anything, it just compares.

Drill 11 · Cracking the codeE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

No key? Try every key.

A shift cipher on this wheel has only 27 possible keys, and a computer can try them all before you finish reading this sentence. Wrap Drill 6 in one more loop and print every shift with its number. The readable line is the answer.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
for shift in range(27):
    result = ""
    for letter in message:
        slot = 0
        for i in range(27):
            if rack[i] == letter:
                slot = i
        slot = slot + shift
        if slot > 26:
            slot = slot - 27
        result = result + rack[slot]
    print(shift, result)
Predict Twenty-seven lines will print. Which number sits beside the readable one, and why that number? Why does result = "" sit inside the outer loop instead of above it?
Line 24, because the message was made with shift 3 and 27 minus 3 is 24. The tally has to be emptied at the start of each shift; put result = "" above the loop and every line would pile onto the one before it. This is why real ciphers use keys far too big to try one by one: a 27-key wheel falls to a loop. Tomorrow you start building the gadget these messages were protecting.

Hinge question · stop here until you're sureE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The wheel with no key

Part 1: trace the letter Z (slot 25) through the wheel with shift = 4. What slot does it land on after the wrap, and what character is that?
Part 2: a message arrives that was encoded with shift 12. What shift decodes it on this 27-slot wheel, and why?

Part 1: slot 2, the letter C. 25 plus 4 is 29, the wrap subtracts 27, and slot 2 is C. If you said slot 29, you skipped the gate; if you said B, you started counting at 1. Part 2: shift 15, because 12 plus 15 is 27, one full turn, and a full turn changes nothing. If both parts landed, the Cipher Room is yours.
Field Mission · The Dead Drop

Encode it, lock it, crack a classmate's

Part A, the dead drop (terminal): your own message in capitals and spaces, your own shift, run through the Cipher Wheel. Write the coded text on a slip of paper. Keep the shift secret.

Part B, the Safe House door (terminal): your own passphrase, a sentinel while gate, and an attempts counter (Drill 7's pattern inside Drill 10's loop), with an f-string report when it unlocks.

Part C, the crack (terminal): trade slips with a classmate. Paste their coded text into Drill 11, find the readable line, and tell them their shift. They never told you; you cracked it.

Suggested start: A, then B, then C, one pattern per run. House law: one change per run, and point at every while's exit line before you run it.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "

# --- Part A: the dead drop ---
message = "THE FALCON LANDS AT NOON"
shift = 4
result = ""
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for i in range(27):
        if rack[i] == letter:
            slot = i
    slot = slot + shift
    if slot > 26:
        slot = slot - 27
    result = result + rack[slot]
print("Dead drop:", result)

# --- Part B: the safe house door ---
passphrase = "nightjar"
attempts = 1
guess = input("Passphrase: ")
while guess != passphrase:
    attempts = attempts + 1
    guess = input("Passphrase: ")
print(f"The door unlocks after {attempts} attempts. Deliver the drop.")
Part C is Drill 11 with your classmate's text pasted into message. Why does attempts start at 1 and not 0? Answer in your Field Notebook.

Middle · meets the standard

Standards, pacing & evidence (teacher panel)

The pacing choice, stated plainly: Day 2 spends all of its novelty on cryptography, and cryptography turns out to be made of the exact patterns the Day 4 game needs: the tally (the game's streak, score, and frame count), the counter with a gate (the game's four-checkpoint lock test), the flat wrap gate (whose held and flipped cousins fence the lock and bounce the target), == and !=, and while with a named exit (the watch loop under every window). Three marks arrive today (==, !=, while) plus one small move, for letter in message, and loops inside loops appear for the first time in Drill 5, where the slot search runs once per letter. Windows are deliberately not on this page; they open on Day 3 with def, which makes Day 3 the heaviest day of the week. That is the price of a cipher day, and it was paid on purpose.

Where recruits actually stall: Block A (Drills 1 to 6, about 55 minutes) stalls at the wrap: recruits who subtract 26 instead of 27 get an off-by-one that never crashes, which Drill 3's sabotage question is there to name. Trace Drill 5 by hand for M, E, E before anyone runs it. Block B (Drills 7 to 11 plus the hinge, about 40 minutes): the Drill 8 question "does the check happen at the top?" is the stall, and the runaway loop needs the stop control found before the run. Block C, the Dead Drop and the Mission Debrief, needs a full 40 minutes; the slip trade is the high point of the day, so protect it. Tell recruits at the start of Day 3 to run pip install pygame; it is not needed today.
