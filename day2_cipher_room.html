Drill 1 · The cipher rackE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

A word is a rack of letters

Yesterday a rack held gadgets. Text is a rack too: every character has a slot number, starting at 0. This rack holds the 26 capital letters and one space, 27 slots. Two new questions come with it: == asks "the same?" and != asks "different?" The terminal answers True or False.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
print(rack[0], rack[4], len(rack))
print(rack[7] == "H")
print(rack[7] == "h")
print(rack[7] != "h")
Predict Three things print on the first line, then three answers. Write all of them. Which slot holds the space?
A E 27, then True, False, True. Slot 7 is H, and a small h is a different letter: the terminal matches exactly (Day 1, Drill 12's rule). The space lives in slot 26, the last one, and it will matter this afternoon. Read != aloud as "is not" and it stops being slippery. One = fills a briefcase; two ask a question.

Drill 2 · The slot searchE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Which slot is this letter in?

A cipher wheel needs a letter's slot number. The program walks every slot with Day 1's patrol and asks the same question at each one; when the gate says yes, it remembers where it was.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
letter = "H"
slot = 0
for i in range(27):
    if rack[i] == letter:
        slot = i
print("Slot of", letter, "is", slot)
Predict How many times does the gate say yes across all 27 passes? What does slot hold at the end, and what would it hold if letter were a small "h"?
Yes exactly once, on pass 7, so slot ends at 7. A small "h" is on no rack, so the gate never says yes and slot keeps its starting 0: the program would quietly treat an unknown letter as an A. The shape, a loop with a gate inside and a briefcase filled by the gate, is the counter with a gate's cousin; you'll meet the counter itself in Drill 12.

Drill 3 · The shiftE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Move every letter the same number of slots

A shift cipher is one addition: take the letter's slot, add the key. Caesar used 3, two thousand years ago, and his enemies never cracked it. Here is the shift on one letter, no safety equipment yet.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
slot = 7
shift = 3
slot = slot + shift
print(slot, rack[slot])
Predict Slot 7 is H. What number and letter print? Now the dangerous question: Y sits in slot 24. What is 24 plus 5, and does the rack have that slot?
10 K: H slides three slots to K, and every letter of a message would slide the same way; that sameness is what makes it a cipher instead of a scramble. And 24 plus 5 is 29, but the last slot on this rack is 26. There is no slot 29. The next drill drives off that edge with the airbags out, so you can hear exactly what it sounds like.

Drill 4 · Sabotage drill: off the edge of the wheelE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

Crash it, read it, repair it with the wrap

Run the broken version first. Y plus 5 asks for slot 29, and Day 1 taught you what a rack does when you reach for a slot it doesn't have.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
slot = 24
shift = 5

A real cipher wheel doesn't fall off its edge; it comes back around. Past slot 26, subtract one full turn of 27. That's a flat gate doing the wrapping. The repair:

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
slot = 24
shift = 5
slot = slot + shift
if slot > 26:
    slot = slot - 27
print(slot, rack[slot])
Predict Broken version: which line of the traceback names the crime? Repaired version: trace 24 plus 5 through the gate; what prints? And if a saboteur changed the 27 to 26, which letters would come out wrong: all of them, or only some?
The last line: IndexError: string index out of range, Day 1's reach-for-a-missing-slot crime with a cipher wheel for a victim. Repaired: 24 plus 5 is 29, the gate fires, 29 minus 27 is 2 C, and Y has wrapped around to C. The sabotaged 26 would be a silent bug: only letters that wrap would land one slot too far, and the program would never complain. A crash is loud and honest; the silent wrap is Thursday's kind of bug. Keep both in your notebook.

Drill 6 · Pattern: the tallyE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

A briefcase that collects, one pass at a time

The most useful pattern in programming: start a briefcase empty, then let a loop add into it, one piece per pass. Programmers call it the accumulator; at this Academy it's the tally. Here it collects letters; with numbers it becomes a running total, which is how the capstone game will count the passes you hold your lock on target.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
result = ""
for i in range(5):
    result = result + rack[i]
    print(result)
Predict Trace result through all five passes in your Field Notebook. What prints on pass 3, and what does the last line show?
Pass 3 prints ABC; the last line is ABCDE. The line result = result + rack[i] reads strangely at first: put into the briefcase its own contents plus one more letter. The empty quotes at the top matter: a tally has to start with something to add to.

Drill 7 · The Cipher WheelE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Encode: the slot search, the shift, the wrap, the tally

Drills 2, 3, and 4 stacked into one machine. One new move: for letter in message: walks a word letter by letter, the way Day 1's loop walked a rack. Inside it, the slot search runs for each letter, so this is a loop inside a loop. Capitals and spaces only.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "MEET ME AT THE SAFE HOUSE AT SEVEN"
shift = 3
result = ""
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for i in range(27):
        if rack[i] == letter:
            slot = i
    slot = slot + shift
    if slot > 26:
        slot = slot - 27
    result = result + rack[slot]
print(result)
Predict Trace the first three letters, M, E, E, through the wheel. What do they become? And what does a space turn into with shift 3?
M (slot 12) becomes P, and both Es (slot 4) become H, so the coded text begins PHH. The space is slot 26; 26 plus 3 is 29, the wrap brings it to 2, and it comes out as C. Every C in the coded text is a space in disguise; that's why the coded line has no spaces at all. Julius Caesar used this exact cipher two thousand years ago; the wheel in your terminal is the same machine.

Drill 12 · DecodeE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Going forward 24 is going back 3

There is no separate decoder. On a wheel of 27 slots, moving forward 24 lands exactly where moving back 3 would. Feed the coded text back into Drill 6 with the complementary shift. Two lines change; everything else is the same machine.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
shift = 24
result = ""
for letter in message:
    slot = 0
    for i in range(27):
        if rack[i] == letter:
            slot = i
    slot = slot + shift
    if slot > 26:
        slot = slot - 27
    result = result + rack[slot]
print(result)
Predict Why 24? A message encoded with shift 10 arrives: what shift decodes it, and how do you know without running anything?
Because 3 plus 24 is 27, one full turn of the wheel, so every letter lands back where it started. A shift of 10 is undone by 17, for the same reason. The rule: the decoding shift is 27 minus the encoding shift. A program that does a new job after one deliberate change is a program you understand.

Drill 8 · The key ceremonyE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

One machine, twenty-six masks

Here is the deepest idea in this room, and it is one sentence long: the enemy can own your machine; the key is the secret. Every spy service on earth runs on it. Watch one message wear two different keys through the very same wheel.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "STRIKE AT DAWN"
for shift in [3, 10]:
    result = ""
    for letter in message:
        slot = 0
        for i in range(27):
            if rack[i] == letter:
                slot = i
        slot = slot + shift
        if slot > 26:
            slot = slot - 27
        result = result + rack[slot]

Predict Same machine, same message: will the two coded lines share any letters in the same position? And the ceremony question: your enemy steals a perfect copy of this program. Why is your message still safe?
Not one position matches; a different key remakes the whole disguise. And the stolen program betrays nothing, because the secret was never the machine: it is the number you and your partner agreed on. Cryptographers state it as a law, and it's over a century old: assume the enemy knows the system. Drill 10 · Meet whileE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The big shift: watch, don't count

A shift of 60 goes around the wheel twice and then some. Drill 4's gate wraps once and stops caring. while is different: it checks its question at the top of every pass and keeps running the indented lines while the answer is yes. for counts; while watches.

slot = 60
while slot > 26:
    slot = slot - 27
    print(slot)
print("Landed on slot", slot)
Predict Trace it in your Field Notebook, one row per check, including the check that fails. What numbers print, and when does the last line finally run?
33, then 6, then Landed on slot 6. Three checks: 60 is more than 26, yes; 33 is more than 26, yes; 6 is not, no, and only then does the last line run. Every while ends on a check that fails. Notice the subtraction is Drill 5's tally running backward; a while is often a tally with a finish line.

Drill 11 · Sabotage drill: the runaway loopE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The loop that never stops, and how to stop it

A while must move toward no. Break that law on purpose, once, so you know the fix. Before you run this, find the stop control: the square stop button in the terminal's corner, or click in the terminal and press Ctrl+C.

slot = 60
while slot > 26:
    slot = slot + 27
    print(slot)
Predict Read the question and the line under it. Will the answer ever become no? What is going to happen when you run this?
Never. slot starts above 26 and only grows, so the question stays yes forever and numbers pour down the terminal until you end it. Stop it now. This is the runaway loop, and every programmer sets one off by accident sometimes. The fix is a habit: before running any while, point at the line that moves it toward no. If you can't find one, don't run it.

Drill 13 · Pattern: the sentinel gateE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The Safe House door: ask until the passphrase is spoken

The classic while pattern: keep asking while the answer is wrong. The exit is a word, not a count, and it's Drill 1's != doing the watching.

passphrase = "nightjar"
guess = input("Passphrase: ")
while guess != passphrase:
    print("The door stays shut.")
    guess = input("Passphrase: ")
print("The door unlocks. Come in.")
Predict Guess wrong twice, then right. How many times does "The door stays shut." print? Which line moves the loop toward no?
Twice, once per wrong guess. The line that moves it toward no is the second input(), inside the loop: it refills guess so the question can change its answer. Delete that line and the door asks once and sulks forever. A passphrase is the simplest key there is: the door doesn't decode anything, it just compares.

Drill 12 · Frequency analysisE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Pattern: the counter with a gate

Codebreakers have a trick that needs no key. In English, E is the most common letter. Count the letters in a coded message and the most common one is probably E in disguise. The tool is a tally that adds 1 instead of a letter, and only when a gate says yes.

coded = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
count = 0
for letter in coded:
    if letter == "H":
        count = count + 1
print("H appears", count, "times")
Predict How many Hs are in the coded text? If H is really E in disguise, what does that tell you about the shift that made this message?
Eight. And H sits three slots past E, so a codebreaker who has never seen the key guesses shift 3 from the count alone. Count C next and you'll find seven: the spaces in disguise, the second giveaway. Real agents strip the spaces out before encoding, for exactly this reason. The counter with a gate is three Day 1 tools in one shape: a loop, a checkpoint inside it, and a briefcase swap inside the checkpoint.

Drill 13 · Cracking the codeE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

No key? Try every key.

A shift cipher on this wheel has only 27 possible keys, and a computer can try them all before you finish reading this sentence. Wrap Drill 7 in one more loop and print every shift with its number. The readable line is the answer.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "PHHWCPHCDWCWKHCVDIHCKRXVHCDWCVHYHQ"
for shift in range(27):
    result = ""
    for letter in message:
        slot = 0
        for i in range(27):
            if rack[i] == letter:
                slot = i
        slot = slot + shift
        if slot > 26:
            slot = slot - 27
        result = result + rack[slot]
    print(shift, result)
Predict Twenty-seven lines will print. Which number sits beside the readable one, and why that number? Why does result = "" sit inside the outer loop instead of above it?
Line 24, because the message was made with shift 3 and 27 minus 3 is 24. The tally has to be emptied at the start of each shift; put result = "" above the loop and every line would pile onto the one before it. This is why real ciphers use keys far too big to try one by one: a 27-key wheel falls to a loop. Tomorrow you start building the gadget these messages were protecting.

Drill 14 · The clerk's error: the double shiftE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

Two locks of the same kind are one lock

A nervous cipher clerk encodes a message with shift 3, then, to be extra safe, feeds the coded text back through the wheel with shift 4. Twice the locking, twice the safety? Read the output against a single shift of 7 before you answer.

rack = "ABCDEFGHIJKLMNOPQRSTUVWXYZ "
message = "ATTACK AT DAWN"
for shift in [3, 4, 7]:
    result = ""
    for letter in message:
        slot = 0
        for i in range(27):
            if rack[i] == letter:
                slot = i
        slot = slot + shift
        if slot > 26:
            slot = slot - 27
        result = result + rack[slot]
    print(shift, result)
    message = message
Predict This prints the message under shifts 3, 4, and 7 separately. Now the clerk's plan: shift 3 first, then shift 4 on the result. Which of the three printed lines will that double encoding match, and what does that mean for the clerk's extra safety?
It matches the shift 7 line exactly, because sliding 3 slots and then 4 more is sliding 7: shifts add. The clerk bought no safety at all; a codebreaker cracks the doubled message as an ordinary shift of 7 and never learns it was two. Stacking the same kind of lock buys nothing, and knowing that is real cryptography. Stacking two different kinds is another story, and it waits in the Cipher Grid side mission on the hub.

Standards, pacing & evidence (teacher panel)

The pacing choice, stated plainly: Day 2 spends all of its novelty on cryptography, and the day is now built as two benches with a real landing between them. The morning (Drills 1 to 8 plus the morning hinge, about 80 minutes) builds the machine and closes on the room's deepest idea, the key ceremony: the machine can be public, the key is the secret. The afternoon (Drills 9 to 14, the hinge, and the Dead Drop, about 90 minutes) is the codebreaker's chair: while arrives here because cracking and door-guarding need it, and cryptanalysis gets three genuine tools, the frequency leak, the patient 27-key crack, and the clerk's-error insight that same-kind locks add into one. Cryptography turns out to be made of the exact patterns the Day 4 game needs: the tally (the game's streak, score, and frame count), the counter with a gate (the game's four-checkpoint lock test), the flat wrap gate (whose held and flipped cousins fence the lock and bounce the target), == and !=, and while with a named exit (the watch loop under every window). Three marks arrive today (==, !=, while) plus one small move, for letter in message, and loops inside loops appear for the first time in Drill 6, where the slot search runs once per letter. Windows are deliberately not on this page; they open on Day 3 with def, which makes Day 3 the heaviest day of the week. That is the price of a cipher day, paid with eyes open.

The scaffold, stated plainly: the paper wheel in the Field Notebook comes first, before any code: three letters encoded by hand and traded. Recruits who have turned the wheel with their fingers read Drill 1 as a machine for a thing they already did. The wrap is met as a repair, not a rule: Drill 3 drives off the rack's edge with a loud crash, and Drill 4 reads the traceback and installs the gate. The morning hinge is a legitimate floor: a recruit who lands it and goes no further today has succeeded, and the afternoon's patterns re-arrive with support in the Safecracker's Ledger side mission. Nothing in the afternoon is prerequisite to Day 3 except while itself.

Where recruits actually stall: the morning stalls at the wrap: recruits who subtract 26 instead of 27 get an off-by-one that never crashes, which Drill 4's sabotage question is there to name. Trace Drill 6 by hand for M, E, E before anyone runs it. In the afternoon, the Drill 9 question "does the check happen at the top?" is the stall, and the runaway loop needs the stop control found before the run. Drill 14's reveal lands best if the room commits out loud to "twice as safe" or "not safer" before anyone runs it. The Dead Drop and the Mission Debrief need a full 40 minutes; the slip trade is the high point of the day, so protect it. Tell recruits at the start of Day 3 to run pip install pygame; it is not needed today.
